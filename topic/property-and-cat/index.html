Property and catastrophe insurance

599 stories · tracked since Aug 9, 2024 · latest Nov 13, 2026

Property and catastrophe insurance covers damage to buildings and belongings, including losses from storms, floods, fires and earthquakes. Insurers pass much of the peak risk to reinsurers and capital markets, so pricing rests on models of rare events.

How the risk is carried and passed on

A policyholder pays a premium to an insurer, which promises to repay the cost of repairing or replacing insured property after a covered event. Because storms, floods and earthquakes hit many properties at once, insurers buy reinsurance, issue catastrophe bonds or use insurance-linked securities to pass on peak losses. Brokers place the risk, catastrophe models estimate it, and state windstorm pools cover areas the private market avoids.

Why it is hard to price and keep available

The hard part is that catastrophe losses are rare, clustered and shifting. Models built on past events lag a changing climate, so prices, deductibles and policy wording are repeatedly reset, and cover is withdrawn in some regions. Capital supply, reinsurance renewal terms and public disaster agencies all feed into whether property can be written at an affordable price. Claims surges after a major event also test any insurer's handling capacity.

Where the activity sits today

The companies in the coverage are mostly brokers, reinsurers, large carriers, modelling firms and rating or government bodies, with a smaller group of software vendors and MGAs. Attention has moved from basic digital distribution towards modelling, reinsurance capacity, core system replacement and automation of policy and claims work. The subject keeps overlapping with claims and underwriting, AI, partnerships and funding, and with the effect of cuts to public weather and disaster agencies.

Parametric cover versus traditional indemnity

Parametric insurance differs from traditional indemnity cover. It pays a fixed amount when a measured trigger, such as earthquake shaking intensity, is reached, instead of after a loss adjuster assesses damage. Payouts are fast and disputes few, but a customer can suffer damage without being paid, a gap called basis risk. It sits beside conventional property policies rather than replacing them.
