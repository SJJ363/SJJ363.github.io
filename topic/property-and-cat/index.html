Property and catastrophe insurance

453 stories · tracked since Aug 9, 2024 · latest Oct 8, 2026

Property insurance covers damage to buildings and their contents, and catastrophe insurance is the slice of it that responds to large natural disasters. It spans homeowners and commercial policies as well as the reinsurance and catastrophe-bond markets that carriers use to spread the risk further.

How risk moves from a policyholder to capital markets

A property policy sits with a primary insurer, which prices the risk of fire, water damage, theft and storm loss on an individual building. For catastrophe exposure the insurer usually cedes part of that risk to a reinsurer, paying a premium in exchange for the reinsurer covering losses above an agreed attachment point. Reinsurers in turn buy retrocession, and increasingly issue catastrophe bonds, so a hurricane in Florida or an earthquake in Japan can end up funded by pension funds and hedge funds through the insurance-linked securities market rather than by a single balance sheet.

Why pricing has become harder to hold steady

Catastrophe losses have grown more volatile as wildfire, severe convective storm and flood exposure spreads into areas that older models underpriced, so insurers and reinsurers have pushed rates up and tightened terms even in years without a single named hurricane landfall. Claims handling has become its own flashpoint: policyholders and regulators have challenged carriers over how wildfire and flood claims are investigated and paid, and litigation over depreciation, additional living expenses and toxin remediation now shapes underwriting decisions as much as the physical risk itself.

Indemnity cover versus parametric triggers

Most property catastrophe insurance is indemnity-based: it pays out according to the actual, assessed cost of the damage, which can take months to determine after a major event. Parametric insurance instead pays a fixed amount once an independently measured trigger, such as a wind speed or an earthquake's magnitude at a given location, is crossed, regardless of the policyholder's real loss. It settles faster and needs no adjuster, which is why it is spreading into markets like flood, agriculture and multinational programs that indemnity cover struggles to reach quickly.

The activity here runs across primary insurers, global reinsurers, specialty brokers and the catastrophe-modelling firms that price the risk for all of them, alongside newer entrants building parametric platforms.
