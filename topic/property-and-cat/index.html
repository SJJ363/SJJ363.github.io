Property and catastrophe insurance

330 stories · tracked since Aug 9, 2024 · latest Sep 4, 2026

Property and catastrophe insurance covers buildings against damage and pools the risk of major disasters like hurricanes and wildfires. The primary policy sits with an insurer or MGA, but the largest losses are typically passed on to reinsurers, catastrophe bonds and other forms of alternative capital.

How risk moves from a policyholder to a reinsurer

A property policy is written by an insurer, often through a broker or managing general agent (MGA) that handles underwriting and claims on the carrier's behalf. Insurers then cede a portion of that risk to reinsurers under treaty or per-risk agreements, and reinsurers can pass pieces of it on again through retrocession or catastrophe bonds sold to investors. What changes hands is a promise to pay after a defined event, priced using catastrophe models that simulate storms, wildfires and earthquakes across a portfolio of properties rather than one building at a time.

Why the underwriting has become so difficult

Catastrophe risk is hard to price because the loss experience of the past is an increasingly unreliable guide to the loss experience ahead: wildfire behaves differently as building density and vegetation change, and a single storm season can erase years of accumulated premium. Carriers that write homeowners and commercial property in exposed regions have described margins as unusually thin even in ordinary years, which pushes more of the risk toward specialist underwriters, MGAs and reinsurance capital willing to hold it at a price. Sound cat modelling and fast, accurate claims handling after an event matter as much as the policy wording itself.

Primary carriers versus reinsurance and cat capital

The subject splits along the chain described above. Primary insurers and MGAs sell policies directly to homeowners and businesses, competing on price, coverage and how quickly they settle a claim after a loss. Reinsurers, and increasingly alternative capital raised specifically for catastrophe risk through instruments like industry loss warranties and cat bonds, sit behind them and absorb the tail. A newer variant sits inside the first group: specialist wildfire and flood underwriters built around their own risk data rather than an insurer's general book, competing on how precisely they can select and price a single property.

What the coverage here shows

The companies most active in this category range from global brokers arranging placement and advising on risk, through rated reinsurers and analytics providers building the catastrophe models the market prices against, to newer wildfire- and homeowners-focused underwriters raising capital of their own. Alongside that sits routine consolidation among brokers and periodic transfers of old reserves from one balance sheet to another, plus experiments with parametric cover that pays out on a measured trigger rather than an assessed loss. This category regularly overlaps with claims and underwriting, funding, and broker M&A, since a catastrophe-exposed book touches all three.
