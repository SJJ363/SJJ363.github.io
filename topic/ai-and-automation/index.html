AI in insurance

1335 stories · tracked since Aug 1, 2024 · latest Oct 23, 2026

AI in insurance is the use of machine learning and generative models to price, sell, underwrite and settle insurance policies. It spans claims triage, underwriting support and software agents that do broker and customer work.

How models and agents are put to work

Insurers and brokers apply machine learning to the data they already hold: applications, policy documents, claims files, images and call transcripts. Older models score risk and flag likely fraud. Newer language models read submissions, summarise medical or property records and draft correspondence. Agents go a step further and carry out a task, such as chasing missing information or filing a claim. The buyers are carriers, managing general agents, brokers and agencies. They buy from vendors, build in-house, or run as AI-native carriers themselves.

Why it matters and what makes it hard

Insurance is an information business. Underwriters, adjusters and brokers spend much of their time reading documents and keying data, so automating that work can cut cost and speed up a quote or a payout. The difficulties are specific to the industry. Pricing and claims decisions are regulated and must be explainable. Training data is often messy or locked in legacy systems. Models can make confident errors, so most deployments keep a person reviewing the decision. Verifying what a model produced is often the hardest step.

Where the activity sits today

The largest brokers and carriers, including Gallagher, Aon, Marsh, WTW, AXA, Zurich and Chubb, appear most often, usually through partnerships, internal programmes and new technology or AI leadership roles. Alongside them are AI-native startups such as Corgi, Outmarket AI and FurtherAI, and software vendors such as Vertafore and Guidewire adding agents to existing platforms. The emphasis has moved from analytics pilots towards agents that perform work in agencies, claims teams and underwriting desks. The same stories keep landing in funding, product launches, claims and underwriting, partnerships and property.

Assistive tools and autonomous decisions

Readers often treat all of this as one thing, but the deployments differ in how much authority the software has. An assistive tool flags a gap in a medical disclosure or summarises a file and leaves the decision to a person. An autonomous system prices or settles on its own. Much of what is announced is the first kind, and executives repeatedly describe spending as aimed at back-office savings, not at rebuilding how insurance is distributed. Fully autonomous underwriting is being piloted in specialty lines but remains the exception.
