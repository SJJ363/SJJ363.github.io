AI in insurance

652 stories · tracked since Aug 1, 2024 · latest Aug 12, 2026

AI in insurance is the use of machine-learning and generative-AI systems by carriers, brokers and MGAs to underwrite, sell and settle claims. The activity ranges from narrow scoring models embedded in existing systems to newer agentic tools that handle a task, such as a claims review or an underwriting decision, largely on their own.

How AI actually enters an insurer's workflow

Three layers do most of the work. Machine-learning scoring models, trained on historical claims and third-party data, price risk and flag fraud inside underwriting systems that already existed. Generative AI, built on large language models, drafts claims correspondence, summarizes policy documents and answers customer questions. Agentic AI goes further, chaining several steps together so a system can review a claim, request more information and reach a decision with only occasional human sign-off. Carriers, brokers, MGAs and specialist software vendors all build or buy some combination of the three.

Why the hard part isn't the model itself

Adoption is easier than deployment. An insurer's core systems and claims workflows are old and were not built to receive an external model's output automatically, so a model that scores risk well can still fail to change how a policy is priced or a claim is closed. Regulators are also starting to ask how these systems are supervised: US state regulators are drafting frameworks for reviewing how carriers use AI in decisions that affect a policyholder, and conduct risk, an AI agent misleading a customer or denying a claim without a clear reason, is now a named concern.

Who is active and how the emphasis has shifted

The largest global brokers, Aon, Willis Towers Watson, Gallagher and Marsh, show up repeatedly, both as users of AI internally and as advisers helping clients weigh the risk AI creates. Carriers such as Zurich and Chubb appear alongside AI-native startups, including Corgi, Pace, Qumis, mea, Outmarket AI and NeuralKart, built specifically around automating underwriting, claims or distribution rather than adding AI to an existing product line. Coverage has moved from describing AI as a product feature toward describing it as a decision-maker, and it now overlaps most with funding news, product launches, claims and underwriting, and partnerships.

Insuring AI, not just using it

A second, separate story runs alongside the first: AI as something insurance must cover rather than something it uses. Data centers built to train and run AI models are large, capital-intensive and hard to price, and brokers now structure bespoke property and casualty programs for them. Professional-liability and technology-errors coverage is being reworked too, since an AI system that gives a customer bad advice or makes a flawed decision creates a liability exposure that existing policy wording was not written to address. The two threads, AI as a tool and AI as a risk, increasingly involve the same brokers and carriers at the same time.
