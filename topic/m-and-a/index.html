Insurance M&A

622 stories · tracked since Aug 6, 2024 · latest Nov 7, 2026

Insurance M&A is one company buying another across insurers, brokers, agencies and insurance technology firms. Deals range from small agency purchases to carrier takeovers, and increasingly include software vendors.

How an insurance acquisition actually works

A buyer acquires a target's shares or assets for cash, stock or a mix, usually after due diligence, and with regulatory approval where a licensed carrier changes hands. The parties are the acquirer, the sellers, often a private equity sponsor, and the regulators who review change of control. Terms a reader will meet include tuck-in, a small agency folded into a larger broker; book of business, the policies and client relationships being bought; and run-off, a closed portfolio of legacy liabilities.

Why buyers pay and where deals go wrong

Insurance rewards scale, data and distribution, so buyers pay for client relationships, underwriting skill or technology that would take years to build. Succession is a steady driver, since founders of independent agencies often sell rather than wind down. The hard parts are valuing long-tail liabilities, keeping producers and clients after the sale because the asset is largely people, integrating old systems, and clearing regulators when a balance-sheet carrier is involved.

What the activity looks like in this archive

Much of the volume is broker consolidation: large brokers and roll-ups such as Gallagher, NFP, Aon and Inszone keep buying regional agencies, often on succession grounds. Alongside that, reinsurers and large carriers buy specialty and cyber underwriters, as with Munich Re and At-Bay or Zurich and Beazley. A newer thread is software, where technology vendors and private equity-backed platforms buy core-system and data providers. These deals keep landing in AI and automation, cyber, and claims and underwriting.

Three kinds of deal that get lumped together

Distribution deals buy clients and producers, and are valued on retained relationships. Carrier and underwriting deals buy risk appetite, capital and licences, so regulators and rating agencies take an interest. Technology deals buy platforms, data or software, and the logic is usually capability rather than premium. A minority investment in a startup is not an acquisition at all and belongs with funding, though outlets often report both in the same language.
