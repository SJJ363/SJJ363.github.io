Insurance claims and underwriting

208 stories · tracked since Aug 5, 2024 · latest Aug 6, 2026

Underwriting is how an insurer decides whether to accept a risk and what to charge for it; claims is how it pays out once a loss happens. The two functions sit on either side of every policy, and are handled by different teams, tools and, increasingly, different vendors.

What underwriting and claims actually do

An underwriter reviews an application, weighs the exposure against actuarial data, and prices, declines or refers the risk before a policy is bound; the premiums collected across that book of business fund the payouts. A claim begins when a policyholder reports a loss, known as first notice of loss or FNOL, then moves through investigation and adjusting toward a settlement, a denial, or subrogation against a third party responsible for the loss. Combined ratio and loss ratio are the scorecards insurers publish for how well both sides performed over a period.

Why both sides are getting harder to run

Underwriting is getting harder because the risks themselves are shifting faster than historical data can describe them: cyber exposure changes with the attacker's tools, not the calendar, and supply chains and climate patterns move in ways a static rating table cannot anticipate. Claims are under separate pressure, from fraud that increasingly uses the same automation tools insurers are adopting to detect it, and from policyholders who expect a settlement on the timeline of a retail transaction rather than a paper file. Regulators are also pushing back on pricing that gets too individual, wary of eroding the risk pooling that makes insurance work at all.

Where the technology is actually landing

Most of the visible activity sits with specialist underwriting agencies and claims platforms rather than the largest carriers rebuilding from scratch: managing general agents launching narrow, well-defined products, and standalone claims and fraud-detection tools that carriers then license or partner with instead of building in house. Large insurers and reinsurers show up mostly as partners and acquirers, buying analytics capability or pairing with an outside AI vendor rather than announcing their own platform. The activity overlaps heavily with property and catastrophe risk, where better modeling changes both what gets underwritten and how a loss gets adjusted afterward.

Underwriting risk versus underwriting fraud

Two distinct problems get called underwriting in practice. One is pricing risk correctly at the point of sale, using better data to judge an individual applicant rather than a broad rating class. The other is catching bad actors after the fact, whether a fraudulent claim or a policy obtained on false information, which is a detection problem rather than a pricing one. The tools and the vendors involved differ even when both get described as AI for insurance.
