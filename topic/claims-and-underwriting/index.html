Insurance claims and underwriting

241 stories · tracked since Aug 5, 2024 · latest Aug 13, 2026

Underwriting is how an insurer decides whether to accept a risk and prices it; claims is the process of paying out once that risk occurs. Both functions sit at the center of an insurer's profitability, and AI is now reshaping how each one is done.

The two jobs inside a policy's lifecycle

Underwriting is the process an insurer runs before a policy is bound: assessing how risky an applicant is, deciding whether to accept the risk at all, and setting the price and terms. Claims is what happens afterward, once a loss occurs — a policyholder reports it, an adjuster or claims handler investigates and verifies it against the policy wording, and the insurer pays, denies or settles. Managing general agents and underwriters (MGAs, MGUs) often underwrite on a carrier's behalf under delegated authority, while actuaries build the pricing models underwriters apply judgment on top of.

Why AI is changing both jobs at once

Underwriting and claims are both being reshaped by artificial intelligence, and the coverage below tracks that shift closely: AI models now score risk, flag fraud, and speed up claims estimates that used to take an adjuster days to work through. That compresses costs on both sides of the policy, which is why insurers report it directly in underwriting results and combined ratios. The harder parts are the risks that do not fit standard underwriting at all — industrial computing hardware, cyber incidents that cascade across a single vendor, and long-tail contamination claims — which is pushing carriers to build new specialty programs rather than stretch existing ones.

Who is building in claims and underwriting

The companies here split into three groups: large diversified carriers such as Zurich, Allianz, Liberty Mutual, Allstate, MetLife and Travelers, which report underwriting and claims results every quarter; specialty underwriters and MGUs such as Acies MGU and Ryan Specialty's Tera, launching narrow programs for risks the standard market will not take; and claims-technology vendors — Genki, Wave Claims, Cheche, Curant.ai, FurtherAI — automating the claims process itself, alongside data providers like Verisk and J.D. Power that sit underneath both functions. Coverage has moved from general modernization toward AI aimed specifically at claims and fraud, and it often overlaps with AI & Automation, Property & Cat and Funding.

Standard underwriting versus specialty and E&S lines

Most personal and small-commercial insurance is underwritten on standard, filed and regulated forms, which is what keeps home and auto pricing fairly uniform. Risks that fall outside those forms — an unusual hardware exposure, a liability class carriers are pulling back from — move into the excess and surplus market, where MGAs and MGUs price more freely under authority delegated by a carrier. Claims follow a similar split: a first-party claim is the policyholder's own loss, a third-party claim is someone else's claim against the policyholder, and the two are handled under different rules for who has to prove what.
