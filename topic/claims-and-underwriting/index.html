Insurance claims and underwriting

279 stories · tracked since Aug 5, 2024 · latest Aug 24, 2026

Underwriting decides what an insurer covers and at what price; claims is the process of paying out after a loss occurs.

How a risk becomes a policy, then a payout

An underwriter reviews an applicant's risk — a driving record, a property's construction and location, a fleet's loss history, a commercial operation's exposures — and decides whether to accept it, on what terms, and at what premium. A large share of that work is delegated rather than done in-house: a managing general agent or underwriter (MGA/MGU) writes business on a carrier's paper under an agreed set of rules, often for a specialty line the carrier doesn't staff itself. Once a policy is in force, a loss triggers a claim: the policyholder reports it, an adjuster or claims handler investigates and values the damage, and the insurer pays out, part-pays, or denies it under the policy's wording.

Why both sides are hard to get right

Underwriting is a forecasting problem: an insurer prices a risk with only partial information, and coverage sold too cheaply in one cycle can turn into years of underwriting losses once the risk shows up as claims — property and catastrophe lines make this visible fastest. Claims work is hard for a different reason: a single flood or wildfire season produces a surge of losses that differ enormously in cause and severity, and handling them as though they were alike is itself a source of mispricing. Fraud, slow investigation and ordinary back-office friction sit inside that same process, at the moment a policyholder is deciding whether the cover was worth buying.

Carrier underwriting versus delegated authority

Not all underwriting happens the same way. A standard carrier employs its own underwriters and keeps the risk on its own balance sheet. Specialty and niche business — wildfire-exposed property, ports and terminals, data-centre hardware — more often runs through an MGA or MGU with delegated authority to bind cover on a carrier's behalf, frequently because that manager has built underwriting technology or expertise the carrier hasn't. The Lloyd's market adds a further layer: a lead underwriter sets the terms for a risk and other syndicates "follow" that lead rather than underwriting it independently, which is why efforts to digitise following are treated as underwriting infrastructure in their own right.

Where the activity is concentrated now

Coverage here leans heavily toward large composite insurers and reinsurers alongside specialty MGAs and claims-services firms, and the activity clusters around AI and automation applied to both halves of the process — underwriting tools that speed up risk assessment, and claims software aimed at faster, more consistent payouts. Executive moves in underwriting and claims leadership appear often, a sign insurers are investing in the people as well as the tools. The subject regularly overlaps with property and catastrophe risk, partnerships between insurers and data or software vendors, and funding rounds for underwriting and claims startups, reflecting how much of current insurtech investment is aimed at these two functions specifically.
