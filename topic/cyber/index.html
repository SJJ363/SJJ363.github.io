Cyber insurance

314 stories · tracked since Aug 28, 2024 · latest Nov 27, 2026

Cyber insurance is cover for the financial losses a business suffers from hacking, ransomware, data breaches and system outages. It also usually pays for the specialist help needed to respond.

What a cyber policy pays for

A cyber policy combines first-party and third-party cover. First-party cover pays the policyholder's own costs: forensic investigation, restoring systems, lost income while operations are down, ransom negotiation and notifying affected people. Third-party cover pays for liability to customers, regulators and partners after a breach. Most insurers attach an incident response line staffed by lawyers and forensic firms, so the first call after an attack goes to a panel the insurer has already vetted. Buyers meet terms such as retention, sublimit, waiting period, war exclusion and dependent business interruption.

Why it is hard to underwrite

Cyber risk differs from fire or theft because losses are not independent. One flaw in widely used software, one cloud provider's outage or one compromised vendor can hit thousands of policyholders at once, so insurers worry about accumulation as much as individual claims. Threats change faster than annual policies renew, and loss history is short compared with other lines. Insurers therefore rely on outside-in scanning, security questionnaires and modelling firms to price risk. Wording is also contested: war and infrastructure exclusions, and whether an outage without an attack is covered, remain unsettled.

Who is active and what is changing

Activity is spread across specialist managing general agents and insurtechs that scan clients' systems, large carriers and Lloyd's syndicates, reinsurers that absorb the peaks, and modelling firms that quantify accumulation. Brokers and surplus lines wholesalers shape how small and mid-sized businesses buy. The emphasis has moved from simply selling policies towards judging security posture, with patching speed, vendor dependencies and artificial intelligence now central to underwriting talk. Acquisitions of specialists by large reinsurers and new parametric products for cloud outages show the market consolidating and experimenting with triggers.

Small businesses and the protection gap

Large companies buy cyber cover routinely, while small and mid-sized businesses remain the main gap. Many owners underestimate their exposure, assume another policy already covers it, or choose on price alone. Some turn to captives or self-insurance funds where commercial limits fall short. Markets also differ: some countries have little more than a statutory minimum, while personal cyber cover for households is still a young product with unsettled pricing and limits. Closing this gap is a main commercial aim for carriers and agents alike.
