Cyber insurance

74 stories · tracked since Aug 28, 2024 · latest Aug 12, 2026

Cyber insurance covers the financial losses a business suffers from hacking, data breaches, ransomware and other digital attacks. Policies are usually sold by specialty carriers or MGAs through a broker, and pair pre-breach advice with post-breach incident response.

How a cyber policy actually works

A business buys a policy from a specialty carrier or a managing general agent, usually placed through a broker rather than bought direct. First-party cover pays the buyer's own costs — forensic investigation, a breach coach who runs the response, customer notification, and lost income during downtime. Third-party cover pays claims and legal defence when someone else — a customer, a regulator — comes after the buyer over a breach. Ransomware terms and a war exclusion for state-linked attacks are two of the most negotiated clauses in the policy.

What makes the risk so hard to price

The hard part is aggregation: one cloud outage or one compromised software vendor can trigger claims from many policyholders on the same day, which is a different shape of loss than a single burglary or fire. Insurers are also watching artificial intelligence work both sides of the risk — as a new attack surface, since staff pasting confidential data into a chatbot creates an exposure with no settled underwriting question yet, and as a tool ransomware groups use to scale attacks. Even so, capacity keeps expanding rather than pulling back, despite carriers openly saying a market-wide loss event is only a matter of time.

Who is writing the cover, and how it is shifting

The names active in this archive split between long-established specialty carriers and reinsurers extending an existing book — Arch, Beazley, AXA XL, Liberty Mutual — and newer insurtechs built around cyber alone, such as BOXX, Coalition, Stoïk and Mitigata. The coverage increasingly overlaps with AI & Automation and Claims & Underwriting, as insurers add specific wording for AI-related loss and lean on automation inside their own incident response. It also turns up inside Property & Cat, where reinsurance brokers have started combining catastrophe and cyber risk in the same structure, and inside Partnerships, as carriers buy or team up with security firms rather than build that expertise in-house.

How the market splits by size and geography

Coverage is not one product. Large enterprises and reinsurers deal in complex, heavily negotiated placements with detailed sub-limits and panel requirements, while a newer wave of insurtechs sell simpler, faster policies aimed at small and mid-sized businesses that could never afford that process. Personal lines are also emerging, extending cover to executives and their households rather than only companies. And the gap is uneven by market — some countries still rely on thin statutory minimums that leave even substantial firms effectively underinsured against a loss most other markets treat as a standard commercial risk.
