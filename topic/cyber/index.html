Cyber insurance

100 stories · tracked since Aug 28, 2024 · latest Aug 20, 2026

Cyber insurance pays out when a policyholder suffers a data breach, ransomware attack or other computer-system failure that costs money to fix. It covers first-party costs like incident response and lost income, plus third-party liability, sold by both specialist MGAs and traditional insurers.

What a policy actually pays for

Cover splits into two halves: first-party costs the policyholder incurs directly, such as forensic investigation, ransom negotiation, system restoration and lost income while systems are down, and third-party liability owed to the people whose data was exposed, pursued as lawsuits or regulatory fines. Insurers increasingly bundle in access to incident-response vendors and monitoring tools before a breach happens, not just payment after one, blurring the line between a policy and a cybersecurity product. Coverage is usually placed through a managing general agent that specializes in the risk rather than the carrier that ultimately holds it on its balance sheet.

Why underwriting it is unusually hard

Unlike a house fire, a single software vulnerability or cloud outage can hit many policyholders on the same day, so losses that look independent turn out to be correlated — part of why cloud-outage cover is now written and priced as its own product rather than folded into a general cyber policy. The threat itself moves faster than an annual renewal cycle: a ransomware technique or phishing method common this year may be obsolete by the next, so insurers lean on continuous monitoring of a policyholder's network rather than a one-time application. Insurers are also starting to underwrite AI itself as a new source of exposure, not only use it as an underwriting tool.

Who is writing it and how the field has moved

The specialist end of the market is made up of MGAs and platforms built specifically for this line, sitting alongside large incumbent carriers, reinsurers and brokers that distribute or reinsure the risk. The clearest recent shift is consolidation: a major reinsurer acquiring one of the specialist underwriting platforms outright, turning what had been a ceding relationship into direct ownership. Alongside that, product design keeps narrowing — parametric triggers for cloud outages, add-on cover for AI-generated deepfake fraud — while claims activity keeps surfacing new angles, including breaches involving employee data at large personal-lines insurers themselves.

Parametric cover versus traditional indemnity

Most cyber insurance is indemnity-based: it pays the actual cost incurred once a covered event happens, and an adjuster verifies the size of the loss. Parametric cyber cover instead pays a fixed amount once a defined trigger occurs, such as a named cloud provider going down for a stated length of time, without a claims investigation — faster to pay, but the cover is only as good as what the trigger actually measures. Cloud-outage products are the clearest example, sold alongside the older indemnity products from the same carriers rather than replacing them.
