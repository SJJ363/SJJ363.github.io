Cyber insurance

203 stories · tracked since Aug 28, 2024 · latest Oct 8, 2026

Cyber insurance is cover that pays a business for losses from hacking, ransomware, data breaches and system outages. It also funds the response, such as forensics, legal advice and notifying affected people.

What a cyber policy pays for and who is involved

A business buys a policy through a broker or directly, and an insurer or managing general agent underwrites it. Cover usually splits into first-party loss, such as incident response, data restoration, business interruption and extortion payments, and third-party liability, such as privacy claims and regulatory defence. Insurers share risk with reinsurers, who also set much of the market's appetite. Buyers meet terms such as retention, sublimit, waiting period and war exclusion.

Why it is hard to underwrite

Cyber losses do not behave like fire or flood. Attackers adapt, so past claims describe a threat that has already changed, and many insured firms rely on the same cloud providers and software, so one failure can hit thousands of policyholders at once. That accumulation risk is the central worry for carriers and reinsurers. Pricing therefore leans on security scans, questionnaires and claims data, and wordings keep being redrawn around exclusions and what counts as a covered event.

Where the activity sits today

The activity is split between specialist cyber underwriters and insurtechs, large global carriers and reinsurers, and brokers building their own programmes. Risk-intelligence and scanning tools sit beside the underwriters, helping them see what a client actually runs. Consolidation has appeared, with a reinsurer taking direct ownership of a cyber platform it had backed. Reinsurance demand is described as steady, and hiring moves between the specialists are frequent.

How artificial intelligence is changing the product

Recent activity has moved from selling basic cover to deciding how AI fits within it. Insurers are rewriting wordings to say whether AI-related losses are covered, and some now offer affirmative AI cover, including for deepfake fraud and intellectual property. Tools that detect AI use inside a client help underwriters judge exposure. Vendor and third-party failures, and the question of what falls between cyber and other policies, run through much of the discussion alongside data-centre risk.
